MASTER PLAN — Dorina Beirut School Wellbeing Partnership Strategy

Parent & Child Emotional Wellbeing Workshops for Schools in Beirut — A-to-Z Positioning, Offers, Outreach, Executive Presence, and Partnership Plan

Prepared: 23 July 2026 · Status: Ready for execution · Anchor document of the strategy package

1. Executive Summary

Dorina has a rare, timely opportunity in Beirut: a documented, urgent need for child and family emotional wellbeing support; a private-school sector that is dollarized and competes on holistic offerings; national institutional momentum behind school mental health; and almost no bilingual, parent-focused, coaching-based, non-clinical independent providers serving schools directly.

The strategy is simple to state and disciplined to execute:

"Dorina helps schools and families strengthen children's emotional wellbeing through practical parent–child support, coaching-based tools, and bilingual workshops adapted to each school community."

We launch under the umbrella Parent & Child Emotional Wellbeing Workshops for Schools in Beirut (premium variant: School–Family Emotional Wellbeing Programs), delivered through three tracks — Parent Workshops, Child Support Workshops, and the flagship School Partnership — priced and toned differently for three segments: premium private/international schools, community-focused schools, and NGO/social-impact programs.

The business model deliberately climbs a ladder: a free or low-cost introductory talk opens the door; a paid workshop proves value; a term package builds rhythm; an annual partnership ($2,500–18,000/year depending on tier and segment, ranges to validate) becomes the core revenue engine. One-off talks are the entry point, never the destination.

Brand-wise, we build Dorina the person first — the trusted bilingual expert schools can meet, hear, and reference — while keeping an institutional initiative (working shortlist led by Nabta | نبتة, trademark checks pending) ready for Phase 2 when school contracts demand an organizational face.

The 90-day window (27 July – 25 October 2026) is timed to the Lebanese school calendar: August for asset building and outreach preparation, September for the back-to-school outreach peak, October for pilot delivery and the first partnership proposals. Success by day 90 means: 30 schools listed, 20 contacted, 8 discovery meetings, 3 pilots delivered, 1 signed partnership, NPS ≥ 8.5, 2 testimonials, a rebuilt LinkedIn presence, and 1 media appearance.

Everything below is built on two guardrails that protect the brand from day one: never lead with hypnotherapy in school-facing copy (credentials appear later as proof), and no clinical or diagnostic claims — Dorina complements school counselors and licensed professionals; she never replaces them.

2. Market Opportunity — Beirut / Lebanon

2.1 The need is documented, recent, and severe

Lebanon's children are carrying an extraordinary emotional load. UNICEF's 2025 child-focused rapid assessment found that after the 2024 escalation, 72% of caregivers reported their children were anxious or nervous, and 62% reported them depressed or sad (UNICEF press release). This is not a niche concern — it is a majority-of-families reality, and it sits on top of years of compounding crisis documented in UNICEF's ongoing Lebanon appeal.

For schools, this shows up daily: anxious children, overwhelmed parents, teachers absorbing emotional labor they were never trained for, and parent–school relationships strained by everyone's stress. Schools know they need help; most lack a practical, parent-facing answer.

2.2 The buyer landscape is unusually private — and dollarized

Lebanon historically has one of the world's highest private-school enrollment shares — roughly 70% pre-crisis, ranked third worldwide in private elementary enrollment, with over 1,600 private schools (The National; The Beiruter). Even after crisis-driven migration toward the public sector, the private segment remains the dominant, decision-capable buyer in Beirut.

Critically, premium Beirut school fees are now largely dollarized, with top-tier institutions (ACS Beirut, International College senior years) charging roughly $14,000–22,000 per year (Tutopiya international fees guide; International College, Beirut). These schools compete for enrollment on holistic offerings — wellbeing programming is a differentiator they can budget for in fresh dollars. A $6,000–10,000 annual wellbeing partnership is a rounding error against a single classroom's fee income, and a visible value-add for admissions marketing.

2.3 Institutional momentum: wellbeing is officially on the agenda

Dorina is not pushing against the current; she is riding it.

  • The Ministry of Public Health's National Mental Health Programme (established 2014) and the National Mental Health Strategy 2024–2030 explicitly include the objective of linking schools to community mental health services (MoPH programme page; Strategy 2024–2030 PDF).
  • MEHE, UNESCO, and Japan have launched a mental health and psychosocial support (MHPSS) program covering 50 public schools and 10,000 students (UNESCO announcement). When the education ministry itself invests in school wellbeing, private schools feel both permission and pressure to act.
  • Child protection has a legal anchor — Law 422 (2002) and MEHE's 2018 child protection policy (End Corporal Punishment country report; UNICEF Lebanon child protection) — giving Dorina a compliance-aligned frame for safeguarding conversations with principals.

2.4 The gap Dorina fills

Put the three facts together: enormous documented need + official momentum + a dollarized private-school sector competing on holistic offerings — and then note what is missing. NGO programming targets vulnerable public-school populations and is grant-cycled. Clinical services are individual, referral-based, and stigma-laden for many families. School counselors are stretched thin and student-facing, not parent-facing. Almost nobody offers what Beirut's private and community schools actually need to buy: a bilingual, parent-focused, coaching-based, non-clinical, school-partnership-ready wellbeing provider who can stand in front of 40 Arabic- and English-speaking parents on a Tuesday evening and give them practical tools.

That is Dorina's lane. She enters it as a complement to counselors, NGOs, and the national strategy — never a replacement — which makes her easy to say yes to.

3. Competitive / Alternative Landscape

Dorina's landscape is best read as allies, adjacents, and alternatives rather than head-on competitors. The strategic posture toward every serious player is partnership or peaceful coexistence.

3.1 NGOs — complementary partners, not competitors

  • Himaya — child protection specialist with established school programs (UNICEF feature). Overlap: child safety awareness in schools. Relationship play: Himaya handles protection cases and awareness campaigns; Dorina handles parent skills and coaching-based wellbeing. Referral pathway both directions.
  • Embrace — national mental health NGO operating the Lifeline 1564 (Lebanon Traveler overview of mental health NGOs). Relationship play: Dorina cites the 1564 Lifeline in every parent handout as her referral line for acute needs — an instant credibility and ethics signal.
  • War Child — psychosocial support for conflict-affected children (War Child Lebanon). Operates mainly in vulnerable/refugee contexts; potential subcontract or co-delivery partner for NGO-track work.
  • Right To Play — play-based learning and resilience programming (Right To Play Lebanon). Methodologically adjacent (skills through experience, not therapy); a model to reference and a possible programmatic partner.

None of these organizations sells paid, parent-facing, bilingual coaching workshops to private schools. They validate the field, absorb the acute/clinical end of need, and make Dorina's non-clinical positioning safer — she can always refer out, by name.

3.2 School counselors — complement, never replace

Every credible Beirut private school has at least one counselor. Dorina's rule, stated in every pitch: "I work alongside your counseling team, never instead of it." Counselors are student-facing, case-loaded, and rarely resourced to run parent education programs. Dorina takes the parent-education and whole-community layer off their plate and refers individual concerns back to them. In practice, counselors become Dorina's strongest internal champions — the pitch should actively request their presence in discovery meetings.

3.3 International parenting influencers

Beirut's educated parents already follow Dr. Becky, Big Little Feelings, and similar English-language accounts. These raise awareness and normalize parenting support — but they are not in the room, not bilingual, not adapted to Lebanese family dynamics or the current crisis context, and cannot partner with a school. They are, effectively, free top-of-funnel marketing for Dorina's premise. Content strategy (§9) should engage with their themes and localize them.

4.3 What we never say

  • Never hypnotherapy-first. Hypnotherapy is never the opening line, headline, or subject line of school-facing material. Credentials appear later as credibility proof, framed as "certified practitioner trainings in coaching and therapeutic modalities": Master in Clinical Hypnotherapy, Master in NLP / Coaching, Master in Time Line Therapy (exact wording, never altered).
  • Never clinical claims. No "diagnosis," "treatment," "therapy for anxiety/depression," "trauma treatment," or medical outcome promises. Approved vocabulary: support, tools, skills, wellbeing, resilience, referral to licensed professionals when needed.
  • Never replacement language. Dorina complements counselors, psychologists, and NGOs. Every deck and proposal includes the referral commitment.
  • Never fear-based marketing. No exploiting crisis statistics to frighten parents; the data frames need, the messaging offers hope and practical capability.

5. Target Segments & ICP Profiles

ICP 1 — Premium Private School Principal / Head of School

  • Goals: enrollment retention and differentiation; a visibly holistic school; calm, cooperative parent body; board-presentable initiatives.
  • Pains: anxious students post-2024, demanding stressed parents, an overloaded counselor, competitor schools advertising wellbeing programs.
  • Buying triggers: back-to-school planning (Aug–Sep), a parent-body incident or complaint wave, accreditation/holistic-education reviews, a peer school's visible program.
  • Objections & answers: "We have a counselor." → "This is the parent-education layer your counselor doesn't have time for — I work with your team, never instead of it." "Budget." → dollarized fee base makes $6–10k/yr defensible as an admissions differentiator; start with one paid workshop. "Is this therapy?" → "No — coaching-based, non-clinical skills workshops, with referral to licensed professionals when needed."

ICP 2 — Community School Director

  • Goals: tangible help for struggling families; parent engagement; affordable, practical programming.
  • Pains: families under economic stress, conflict at home spilling into classrooms, no budget for a full counseling department.
  • Buying triggers: parent-committee requests, a low-cost pilot offer, a trusted referral from another director.
  • Objections & answers: "Parents won't pay / can't pay." → school-paid community pricing ($200–400/workshop) or parent-association co-funding; token-fee intro session ($150–300). "Will it be in Arabic?" → yes — natural, warm Arabic, not translated slides. "Is it preachy?" → practical, tool-per-session format; parents leave with things to do tonight.

ICP 3 — NGO Program Manager (education/child protection portfolios)

  • Goals: deliver funder KPIs on MHPSS/parenting-support outputs; credible, safeguarding-compliant facilitators; measurable session feedback.
  • Pains: thin facilitator bench for bilingual parent work; donor reporting burden; trauma-sensitivity requirements.
  • Buying triggers: a funded project needing a parenting-skills component; a gap left by a departing facilitator; alignment with the MoPH 2024–2030 strategy language in proposals.
  • Objections & answers: "What's your safeguarding setup?" → written safeguarding statement, staff-present delivery, consent forms, Law 422-aligned practice. "Rate?" → per-project quotes tied to funder budgets, sliding scale — structured, never free-unlimited. "Evidence?" → pre/post feedback forms, NPS, attendance data packaged for donor reports.

ICP 4 — Parent Association / Parent Committee Head

  • Goals: visible value for member families; well-attended events; a "win" to show the school administration.
  • Pains: low event turnout, generic speakers, no budget line of their own.
  • Buying triggers: planning the year's parent-event calendar (Sep–Oct), word-of-mouth from a parent who attended a Dorina session elsewhere.
  • Objections & answers: "We usually get free speakers." → the free/low-cost intro talk is exactly for them — and it converts into school-paid programming. "Language mix in our parent body?" → bilingual delivery is the default, not an add-on. Role in the ladder: the parent association is often the door, while the principal is the budget — treat them as champions, not buyers.

6. Offer Architecture Summary

Full catalog, session outlines, and pricing logic live in OFFER_ARCHITECTURE.md. The structure:

Three tracks (fixed names):

  1. Track 1 — Parent Workshops: communication, emotional regulation at home, discipline without conflict, supporting anxious children, confidence/self-esteem, school stress, emotional expression.
  2. Track 2 — Child Support Workshops: self-confidence, emotional expression, stress & anxiety support (non-clinical), focus & motivation, social skills, resilience, positive identity. Always delivered with school staff present, with parental consent, per safeguarding policy.
  3. Track 3 — School Partnership (flagship): monthly parent sessions, child wellbeing workshops, teacher–parent collaboration sessions, awareness campaigns, custom support plans, an annual wellbeing calendar.

8. Go-to-Market Engine

8.1 Channels, in priority order

  1. Warm introductions — the highest-converting channel in Beirut's relationship-driven market. Map every personal connection to schools: friends who are parents, former colleagues, family networks, alumni ties. Ask each for one named introduction to a principal, counselor, or parent-committee head.
  2. WhatsApp — Lebanon's default professional channel. Short, respectful voice-note-friendly outreach after a warm intro; never cold-blast. Scripts in SCHOOL_OUTREACH_PLAYBOOK.md.
  3. Email — the formal layer for premium schools: a two-paragraph note plus the one-pager PDF, referencing the intro or a specific school signal (a wellbeing week, a new counselor hire, an accreditation push).
  4. LinkedIn — where principals and NGO program managers can verify Dorina before replying. The rebuilt profile (§9) is the landing page for all outreach.
  5. School events & conferences — back-to-school fairs, parent evenings, education conferences: attend, offer to speak, collect counselor and principal contacts in person.

8.2 The pilot-to-partnership ladder

Free / low-cost intro talk  →  Paid workshop  →  Term package  →  Annual partnership
      (door opener)             (proof of value)    (rhythm)          (flagship)

Rules of the ladder:

  • The free intro is strictly limited (first 3–5 premium schools; token fee elsewhere) and always delivered with a feedback form, photos (with consent), and a scheduled follow-up meeting — it is a sales asset, not charity.
  • Every paid workshop closes with a next-step conversation booked before Dorina leaves the building: "Based on today's feedback, here's what a term of this looks like."
  • Term packages (e.g., one session per month for a term) are the bridge product — low commitment for the school, calendar density for Dorina.
  • The annual partnership proposal is presented with the school's own data: their parents' feedback scores, attendance, and quotes from the pilot and term phases.

8.3 Referral loops

  • Parent → school: every workshop ends with a one-line ask on the feedback form — "Would you recommend this to another school community? Which one?" Named referrals feed the target list.
  • Counselor → counselor: Beirut's counselors know each other; a thank-you note and offer of a free resource pack turns each counselor into a broadcaster.
  • NGO → school and school → NGO: cross-referrals with Himaya/Embrace-type partners (they refer parent-education needs to Dorina; she refers cases to them) deepen both credibility and deal flow.
  • Testimonial engine: two school testimonials by day 90 (KPI), each requested within one week of a successful delivery while enthusiasm is fresh.
