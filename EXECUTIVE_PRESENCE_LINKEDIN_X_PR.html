1. Executive Presence Strategy: What a "Credible School-Facing Expert" Looks Like in Beirut

School principals in Beirut are gatekeepers under pressure. They are approached weekly by vendors, trainers, and "coaches" of uneven quality, while managing dollarized budgets, anxious parent committees, and a student body still carrying the weight of crisis years. Before any principal books Dorina, they will do three things: Google her, open her LinkedIn, and ask a counselor colleague, "Do you know her?" Executive presence, for Dorina, is the deliberate engineering of what those three checks reveal.

The credible school-facing expert in Beirut has a very specific profile:

  • Bilingual authority. She moves naturally between Arabic and English — because Beirut's school communities do. A profile that is English-only reads "expat consultant"; Arabic-only reads "not for the international schools." Dorina's presence must visibly carry both, mirroring her bilingual workshop delivery.
  • Warmth + rigor, held together. Parents need to feel she is safe and kind; administrators need to see structure, outcomes, and professionalism. Every asset — headline, photo, post — must pass both tests at once. Warm without rigor is a "nice lady who does talks"; rigor without warmth is a consultant parents won't open up to.
  • Visible ethics and safeguarding. In a market where child protection is codified (Law 422 of 2002; MEHE's 2018 child protection policy), the expert who volunteers her safeguarding practices — consent forms, school staff always present in child workshops, referral pathways to licensed professionals — signals institutional maturity that competitors rarely show. Ethics is not the fine print; it is a differentiator we display.
  • Real classroom proof. Beirut is a small-network city. One photo of a real workshop in a real school hall (with consent, per §4 below), one named testimonial from a school counselor, one parent quote — these outweigh a hundred motivational graphics. The presence strategy exists to manufacture and circulate proof, not opinions.
  • Complementary, never competitive, with counselors. Dorina's public voice must repeatedly and explicitly position her as the counselor's ally. School counselors are both the most influential internal referrers and the people most likely to block an outsider who looks like a threat.

The Five Presence Principles

# Principle What it means in practice What it forbids
1 Lead with the child, not the modality Every headline, bio, and post opens with children's confidence, parent–child communication, or school–family partnership. Credentials appear later as proof ("training includes…"). Never open any asset with "hypnotherapy" or any technique name. No modality jargon in first lines, ever.
2 Bilingual by design Alternate and pair AR/EN content; Arabic in natural, warm Modern Standard Arabic. Name field, banner, and bio all signal both languages. No machine-literal Arabic; no English-only presence.
3 Proof over promises Each month must produce at least one new proof asset: workshop photo (consented), testimonial, feedback statistic ("94% of parents said they'd use the tool this week"). No unverifiable claims, no outcome guarantees, no clinical or diagnostic language ("treats anxiety," "heals trauma").
4 Safeguarding out loud Publicly narrate the ethics: consent-first photography, staff-present child sessions, referral to licensed professionals. One post per month touches ethics explicitly. Never share a child's face or story without documented consent; never imply replacement of counselors or psychologists.
5 Hope-forward, never exploitative The Lebanon context (UNICEF data, MoPH strategy) is cited to explain why this work matters and what families can do — always ending in agency and practical hope. No crisis voyeurism, no fear-based marketing, no using children's suffering as a hook.

These five principles are the editorial filter. Any post, pitch, or interview answer that violates one of them does not ship.

★ LI-1 (Day 3, LinkedIn, EN — Pillar A):

Your child slams the door. Or throws the tablet. Or says the sentence that makes your blood boil.

What you do in the next 10 seconds matters more than the next 10 minutes.

Here is the 10-Second Rule I teach parents in my workshops:

When your child does the thing — before you speak, before you move — take one slow breath and silently name what YOU are feeling. "I'm angry." "I'm embarrassed." "I'm scared."

That's it. Ten seconds.

Why it works: children don't learn emotional regulation from our lectures. They learn it from watching our nervous system. A parent who pauses is teaching self-control more powerfully than any punishment ever will.

And here's the part parents love: the rule doesn't require you to be calm. It only requires you to be ten seconds slower than your first reaction. Most of us can find ten seconds — even on a Tuesday night, even after a long Beirut workday, even when the homework still isn't done.

Try it this week. Then notice what your child does differently when you respond instead of react.

I share one practical parent–child tool like this every week — in Arabic and English. Follow along if that's useful for your family.

What's the moment that tests your ten seconds the most? Tell me in the comments — I read every one.

★ LI-2 (Day 10, LinkedIn, EN — Pillar C):

To every school counselor in Beirut: I'm not here to replace you. I'm here to make your September easier.

When I tell people I run wellbeing workshops for school communities, some assume that puts me in competition with school counseling teams. Let me be clear about how I actually work:

Counselors hold the deep, ongoing, confidential work with students. That is their profession and their protected space. I don't enter it.

What I do sits one layer out: I work with the PARENTS — the forty adults in the hall who shape a child's emotional world every single evening. Communication tools. Emotional regulation at home. ★ LI-3 (Day 24, LinkedIn, EN — Pillar A):

"How was school?"
"Fine."

Every parent knows this wall. Here's how to take it down — without interrogating your child.

The problem isn't your child. It's the question. "How was school?" asks a tired brain to summarize seven hours into one word. So it does: "Fine."

Swap it for one of these three, and watch what happens:

  1. "What made you laugh today?" — starts with emotion and memory, not evaluation. Even a bad day usually had one funny moment.

  2. "Who did you sit with at lunch?" — concrete, specific, zero pressure. Social information flows naturally from it.

  3. "What was the hardest part of today?" — this one is gold, but only if you can hear the answer without fixing, judging, or panicking. Just: "That sounds hard. Tell me more."

Two rules make these work: ask ONE, not all three (this is connection, not an interview), and share your own answer first sometimes — "The hardest part of my day was the traffic on the ring road" teaches children that everyone has hard parts, and naming them is normal.

The goal of the car ride home isn't information. It's the message underneath: "Your inner world is welcome here."

Which question will you try this afternoon?

7.4 Interview Prep: 10 Likely Questions with Safe, On-Positioning Answers

  1. "What exactly do you do?" — "I help schools and families strengthen children's emotional wellbeing — through practical parent–child tools, coaching-based workshops for parents and children, and year-round partnerships with schools, in Arabic and English."
  2. "Is this therapy?" — "No, and that distinction matters to me. Therapy is clinical work done by licensed mental health professionals. What I do is education and coaching: practical skills for everyday family life — communication, confidence, emotional expression. When I meet a family that needs clinical support, I refer them to licensed professionals. Both are needed; they're different layers."
  3. "What about hypnotherapy — you're trained in it, aren't you?" — "My training includes Master in Clinical Hypnotherapy, Master in NLP / Coaching, and Master in Time Line Therapy. In school and family settings, I don't do clinical work of any kind — I draw on that training the way a chef draws on culinary school: it shapes the quality of simple, practical, fully transparent tools parents and children can use in daily life. Nothing I do in a school is therapy, and nothing happens without full parental consent and school staff present."
  4. "Aren't you replacing school counselors?" — "The opposite — counselors are my closest allies. They hold the deep, confidential work with students; I work one layer out, mainly with parents. Schools tell me parent workshops actually send families to the counselor earlier and more open."
  5. "Can you 'fix' an anxious child?" — "I'd never use that word. Anxiety in children is communication, not a defect. I give parents and children skills to understand and support it — and where anxiety is severe or persistent, the right path is a licensed professional, and I say so plainly."
  6. "Why is this needed in Lebanon now?" — "The data is clear — UNICEF found large majorities of caregivers reporting anxious, sad children after the recent crisis years — and the state agrees: the National Mental Health Strategy 2024–2030 explicitly connects schools to wellbeing support. But the real answer is hope: Lebanese parents show up in numbers when you offer them practical tools instead of lectures."
  7. "Are you criticizing Lebanese parenting?" — "Never. Lebanese parents are among the most devoted I've seen anywhere — many are parenting through conditions most countries can't imagine. I don't correct parents; I equip them."
  8. "What results can you prove?" — "I measure every workshop: parent feedback scores, whether parents report using the tools at home, and school re-booking. I'm careful never to promise clinical outcomes — I promise practical skills, and I publish what parents say."
  9. "How do you protect children in your workshops?" — "Three non-negotiables: school staff are always present in child sessions, every child has documented parental consent, and I follow each school's safeguarding policy — in the spirit of Lebanon's child protection Law 422. Also: no photos of children, ever, without explicit consent."
  10. "What should a parent do tonight?" — [Always close with one tool — e.g., the three-question car-ride swap or the 10-second pause.] "Small, repeated, warm — that's how children's confidence is actually built."

Red-line reminders for any interview: never diagnose from anecdotes; never comment on a specific named child or school incident; never lead an answer with a modality name; if pushed on politics/sect, bridge to "children's wellbeing is the one thing every community in Lebanon agrees on."
